How to use this

Start here

This document explains one forecasting model from the ground up and then uses it on one live question: will FEWS NET classify any area of Sudan as experiencing Famine before 2027? It assumes you know nothing about systems models or about famine classification. It finishes at the point where you could defend the number in a room, and say which assumption would move it.

Part one is the concept, built in nine steps. Part two is the prediction. Each figure is drawn to make one mechanism visible. Most are plotted from the model's own output; the four that are schematic say so in their caption.

The one sentence

A shortage is a cause, and causes take time to travel. Scarcity Equilibrium writes down where a shortage is, where it goes next and how long it takes to get there. The Sudan forecast below is mostly a statement about time.

Part oneThe concept
Section 01first principles

Why shortages are hard to forecast

Start with the unit. A forecaster is usually asked about one thing: a price, a vote, a declaration. Shortages are awkward because the thing you are asked about is almost never the thing that broke first. Famine is declared on deaths and wasting. It starts with roads, rain and money.

Three properties that force all the machinery
1 · Coupled

Domains lean on each other

Food depends on access, access on security, survival on nutrition. A shock to one is a shock to all of them eventually.

2 · Lagged

Each link takes time

Wasting does not kill on the day food runs out. Every link has a delay, and the delays add up along the chain.

3 · Thresholded

Small changes do nothing

Systems absorb small wobbles. Only a move past some tolerance starts dragging the neighbours.

Put those together and simple extrapolation fails. A headline about hunger tells you one domain moved. It does not tell you whether the move is big enough to travel, or how long it will take to reach the domain the question resolves on.

The model is a map of dependencies with a clock on every arrow. Everything else in part one is the rules for how a shortage moves along it.
Section 02the scale

Putting a famine on a line from 0 to 1

You cannot compare a death rate with a food price until both are on the same scale. So every domain gets a score x between 0 and 1. Zero is severe shortage, 0.5 is normal, 1 is a large surplus. The choice matters for how big an effect looks; it does not change which way anything moves.

The IPC scale that FEWS NET maps use is already an ordered scale of shortage, so it maps onto the bottom half of the line. Famine needs three things to be true in the same place at the same time, which is the single most important fact for the forecast.

Section 03the dead-band

Why most bad news fizzles

How hard does one domain drag another? The model answers with an influence function. Inside a tolerance window (the dead-band, here ±0.05 around normal) a domain passes on nothing. Outside it, the pull grows with the gap and then flattens out, because a domain in total collapse cannot drag its neighbours infinitely hard.

The steepness of that curve is a parameter called sharpness. It is one of the numbers nobody can measure directly, which is why section 11 tests what happens when it changes.

Worth knowingThe dead-band is why a bad week rarely becomes a famine. It is also why a slow, steady decline can be invisible to the model until it crosses the edge, at which point the influence switches on all at once.
Section 04links and lags

The order in which things break

Lags turn a map into a schedule. Hit the model with every shock at once (a siege, an aid cut and a surge of displaced people) and watch each domain fall. Security goes first, because that is where the shock lands. Deaths go last, because they sit at the end of the longest chain of delays.

Section 05loops

What makes a crisis feed itself, and what stops it

Follow the arrows and some paths come back to where they started. Those are loops. Multiply the signs along a loop: positive means reinforcing (it makes itself worse), negative means balancing (it corrects itself). Multiply the strengths and you get the loop's gain. A reinforcing loop with gain above 1 runs away; below 1 it amplifies a push and then settles.

LoopTypeActivation gainLag (months)
Worth knowingNo loop in this model has gain above 1. So the model does not predict a runaway collapse; it predicts that a large enough push lands the camps in a new, lower equilibrium. Whether that equilibrium is below the Famine line is what the Monte Carlo in section 09 counts.
Section 06adaptive set points

The new normal

This is the framework's own idea, and it is the one most models leave out. Each domain is pulled back toward a normal level, its set point. But the normal is not fixed. When everything around a place stays poor for long enough, what counts as normal there drifts down to match.

The figure isolates the effect. A camp starts at the old normal of 0.5. Its neighbours sit at 0.3 and never recover. Nothing pushes the camp directly; only its sense of normal is allowed to move. Within a year it has settled at a lower level and treats it as home.

After three and a half years of war, Darfur's camps are like this. Left alone, they do not drift back toward peacetime levels; they hover at a crisis level. So the Sudan model anchors each domain at today's crisis level, not at 0.5.

The model measures shortage against what people have adapted to. A place that has adapted to misery only registers a new deterioration.
Section 07a trap we hit

A shock that never ends

The first version of this model said famine was impossible, and it was wrong for an instructive reason. It treated a surge of displaced people as a one-off kick to food. Every domain is pulled back toward its set point, so the kick washed out within weeks.

A displacement surge is not a kick. More mouths on the same food is a lasting change. The obvious fix, lowering food's normal, is also wrong: the model passes on influence by distance from normal, so a domain whose normal fell with it passes on nothing, and starving people look normal. The right fix is a separate driver that does not recover and keeps pushing.

Worth knowing for Q&AThis is a general rule for the framework, not a Sudan detail. Anything that persists (a siege, a sanction, a closed pipeline) must enter as a driver. Model it as an initial shock and the system will forget it.
Section 08objective vs reported

Reality and the report are two different events

The framework keeps two layers apart: what is happening and what is perceived or recorded. For this question the gap is decisive. The question does not resolve on famine. It resolves on a FEWS NET map showing Phase 5, with a report date before 1 January 2027.

Assessment and publication take weeks, so conditions have to exist by about 1 December to appear on a map dated in time. The model's real horizon is six weeks, not eleven.
Section 09monte carlo

From one run to a probability

A single run answers "what if". A forecast needs "how often". Nobody knows the link strengths exactly, nobody knows which shocks will hit, and the world adds noise. So the model is run 1,500 times. Each run draws its own link strengths (±35%), its own shocks (displacement surge 30%, siege 30%, aid cut 25%, each taken from FEWS NET's named downside scenarios) and its own noise. Counting the runs that hit famine conditions turns the model into a probability.

The figure already shows the answer. Plenty of runs fall below the Famine line. Almost none do it before December. The question is not really whether the crisis worsens. It is whether it worsens fast enough.

Part twoThe prediction
Section 10the question, exactly

Which questions earn the model

Most forecasts go wrong by answering a slightly different question from the one that will be scored. Here is the one being scored, word for word in substance:

Metaculus · question 45464 · 152 forecasters · community 25%

Resolves YES if any FEWS NET classification map with a "Reported" date after August 2026 and before January 2027 shows at least one area, urban settlement or displacement settlement in IPC Phase 5: Famine for the current period. Phase 4 areas marked "!" (would likely be Phase 5 without assistance) do not count.

The model only earns its place when a question has all four of the properties from section 01 plus a feedback loop. On 16 October five open Metaculus questions were forecast. Only one passed.

Section 11the timing curve

The answer is a date, not a yes or no

Take the 1,500 runs and ask, for each month ahead, what share have reached famine conditions by then. That cumulative curve is the model's whole forecast. Read it against the two deadlines from section 08.

Split by branch, the picture is starker than the average. In this model any one of the three shocks is enough to reach famine conditions within about four months, and no run without a new shock gets there at all. The 61% plateau is simply the chance that at least one shock hits. So the model's uncertainty is about whether a shock lands and how fast the chain runs, not about whether a shocked camp tips.

A limitation to say out loudA model where every shocked camp tips is too tidy. Real aid surges, migrations onward and harvest variation would let some shocked camps hold. That is a second reason the model enters the forecast as evidence at modest weight rather than as the answer.

The weakest link in that statement is the lags. They are judgement, not measurement. So the same 1,500-run experiment was repeated with every lag halved, cut by a quarter and stretched by half.

The model is confident about one thing and unsure about another. It is confident famine conditions will not form in the camps it models before December, even with every lag halved. It is unsure how soon after that they come.
Section 12from model to number

Why the model's near-zero is not the forecast

A model that says 0.1% should not be submitted as 0.1%. This one watches the assessed camps. It does not model besieged towns such as Kadugli and Dilling, where FEWS NET says some households already face Phase 5 and survey access is poor. That is the framework's "omitted node" risk, and it is exactly where a surprise classification would come from.

So the cascade enters as one piece of evidence among several. Each piece is a likelihood ratio: how much more likely this fact is in a world where the answer is YES. Ratios multiply the odds, which means they add on a log-odds scale. Starting from the base rate, the ledger below walks to the inside view.

Section 13the forecast

Meeting the crowd

The inside view does not stand alone. The Metaculus community is an aggregate of many forecasters with a good record, so it enters as a strong prior. The two are fused on the log-odds scale, each weighted by how much it is trusted: the community at 4, the inside view at 1.5. A confident inside view moves the result; it cannot take it over.

One decision still openThe pre-registered number applies a calibration slope of 0.9 after fusion, which pulls every answer toward 50%. Because the community is already calibrated, that pull lands the final above both inputs on some questions. With the slope at 1.0 the forecast is . Referenceglossary

Glossary

Activation gainA loop's strength at the moment it switches on. Above 1 with reinforcing sign means runaway.
Balancing loopA loop whose signs multiply to negative. It pulls a system back toward where it was.
Base rateHow often this kind of thing has happened before. The forecast's starting point.
Calibration slopeA post-fusion adjustment on the log-odds scale. Below 1 pulls toward 50%.
Dead-bandThe tolerance window inside which a domain passes on no influence.
Driver nodeAn external push that does not recover on its own. The right way to model a lasting shock.
FEWS NETFamine Early Warning Systems Network. Publishes the maps this question resolves on.
GAMGlobal acute malnutrition: the share of young children who are wasted. Famine threshold 30%.
IPCIntegrated Food Security Phase Classification, the five-phase scale from Minimal to Famine.
Likelihood ratioHow much more likely a fact is if the answer is YES than if it is NO.
Log-oddsThe scale on which independent evidence adds. Probability 50% is 0; 12% is about −2.
Monte CarloRunning the model many times with uncertain inputs drawn at random, and counting outcomes.
Reinforcing loopA loop whose signs multiply to positive. It makes a change bigger.
Set pointWhat counts as normal for a domain. In this framework it drifts with its surroundings.
